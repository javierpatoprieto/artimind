A baroque palace buried on Mars, and someone still dressing for the occasion.

This is a four-frame concept series — no client, no brief, no brand. It exists as an exercise in one thing only: holding a decision across a sequence. Below are the rules I set myself before generating a single image, and the four prompts in full.

## The system

Before anything else, four constraints. Everything that follows obeys them.

**One palette.** Oxide red, salmon, tarnished gold, deep black. Nothing else is allowed into the frame. Not a blue, not a green, not a chrome highlight. A palette is only a palette if you refuse things.

**One light.** Low and raking, always. Never overhead, never diffuse. The long shadows are the second subject of every frame — they do as much compositional work as the architecture does.

**Dust in all four frames.** This is the load-bearing decision. Suspended dust acts as a natural diffuser, softens the falloff, and catches the low sun so the air itself becomes visible. It is also the single thing keeping the series from reading as science fiction: sci-fi renders are clean, and dust is what makes an image feel photographed.

**The gown is never clean.** Dust settles into every fold of the taffeta, into the seams, on the skin. A pristine couture gown in a desert reads as a composite. A dirty one reads as a story.

## The sequence

Wide, then her, then the detail, then absence.

The first frame establishes scale — the ruin is enormous and she is small inside it. The second gives her authority: seated, frontal, unbothered. The third goes down to the hem and the dust, because a sequence needs one frame where you can almost feel the material. And the fourth is the one that makes the series work: she walks away, and all that is left is the train's trail across the sand.

Three frames of ruin, one of absence.

That last frame is the whole method in miniature. A sequence isn't four good images — it's one decision repeated three times and broken once. The repetition builds the world; the break is what turns it into a story.

## Notes for anyone rebuilding it

If your version drifts, it will almost always be one of three things. The palette let something in — a cool highlight, a blue sky, a green tone. The light went soft or overhead somewhere in the middle of the series. Or the dust disappeared, and with it the atmosphere that was holding four separate images together as one place.

Consistency is not a bonus in a campaign. It is the requirement. Everything else is decoration.
